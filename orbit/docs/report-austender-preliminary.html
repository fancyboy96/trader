Australian Government Procurement — AusTender Dataset

Source: AusTender OCDS API (api.tenders.gov.au/ocds)  ·  Period: 1 Jul 2022 – 6 Apr 2026 (FY2023, FY2024, FY2025, FY2026 partial)  ·  Scope: All Commonwealth agencies  ·  Generated: 6 April 2026
Introduction
Australian Government procurement totals $227.0B across 221,633 contracts from FY2023 to April 2026. The structure of that spend is highly concentrated: 514 contracts (0.2% by count) account for $136.6B, or 60% of all value. Understanding Commonwealth procurement means understanding a small number of very large awards.

Defence accounts for $127.0B (56% of total spend), or 56 cents in every procurement dollar. Home Affairs ($11.7B), Social Services ($6.3B), and the two Health portfolios ($13.5B combined) each represent credible standalone markets, though none approaches Defence's scale. Any contractor seeking Commonwealth revenue at scale must either hold a position in Defence or aggregate across multiple civilian agencies.

The contractor base reflects this structure. The top 20 spans defence platform primes (Austal, Hanwha, Boeing, Lockheed Martin, BAE Systems, Anduril), base and estate operators (Cushman & Wakefield, Spotless, Compass, Evolve FM, Ventia), fuel and logistics (Viva Energy, Toll), and enterprise IT (IBM, Accenture, Data#3). Adding FY2023 substantially reshapes the rankings: Lockheed Martin moves from 15th to 7th ($1.28B in FY23 alone), BAE Systems enters the top 10 ($1.04B in FY23), and IBM's $858M FY23 spend exceeds its combined FY2025–FY2026 total. The Big Four consulting firms hold a combined $2.0B across four years; Accenture ($1.3B) leads with KPMG ($650M) second.

Procurement method is the structurally significant finding for market access. 58% of contracts by count are limited tender: direct award to a single supplier with no open competition. Incumbency is the primary mechanism by which Commonwealth spend flows. A supplier without an existing Commonwealth relationship faces a structural barrier.

The majority of Commonwealth procurement, by contract count, bypasses open competition entirely. 58% of contracts are limited tender: agency selects a supplier directly, no public market, no evaluation. The 42% that proceeds to open tender carries higher average contract values ($1.31M vs $814k for limited). Agencies reserve competitive process for their largest and most complex procurements. For most transactions, direct award is the default.

IBM leads by total value at $1.6B, driven by an outsized FY2023 ($858M) — more than its combined FY2025–FY2026 spend. This pattern reflects large multi-year platform awards booked upfront in FY2023, including Home Affairs border and identity systems and Defence IT infrastructure. The underlying run-rate is declining sharply year on year.

Accenture is the largest advisory firm at $1.3B. Its FY2025 spike ($622M) reflects major Health digital transformation platforms — it has moved decisively beyond Defence into health and social services portfolios. An average contract value above $9M reflects systems integration scale, not advisory engagements.

KPMG leads by contract count with 968 engagements over four years at an average of $671k. The profile spans advisory, assurance, and project management across many agencies. Broad reach with a dispersed client base and no dominant account.

PwC ($201M) and Scyne Advisory ($93M) share an ABN — PwC Government Advisory rebranded as Scyne Advisory in October 2023. Contracts are split by award date: pre-October 2023 to PwC, post-October 2023 to Scyne. PwC's $187M in FY2023 reflects the full weight of its government advisory practice before the restructure; the sharp drop in FY2024 ($13M) marks the effective handover. Scyne's profile ($19M FY24 → $57M FY25) shows a practice rebuilding post-rebranding.

MBB penetration is negligible. McKinsey holds $40M. BCG registers $150M, of which 77% is a single $115M DFAT Australian Aid Program contract (FY2025). Bain registers $0.4M. Stripped of the BCG outlier, combined MBB spend over four years is approximately $55M.

Cognizant's absence ($50k, one contract) is informative. It operates below the AusTender threshold as a subcontractor, almost certainly inside IBM and Accenture programmes on major Commonwealth IT platforms. Its true exposure is invisible in this dataset and material in practice.
Data Limitations
1. No subcontractor visibility
AusTender captures only the prime awardee. A $500M systems integration contract may route through fifty subcontractors, none visible here. Supplier analysis based on AusTender alone structurally understates the market position of firms that operate predominantly as second-tier integrators. Cognizant is the clearest example: a minimal AusTender footprint alongside significant known Commonwealth activity through IBM and Accenture programmes.
2. Pass-through accounts must be excluded
The raw dataset's largest entry, FMS Account Reserve Bank of Australia ($23.3B across 160 contracts, FY2023–FY2026), is the US Foreign Military Sales channel: Australian government purchases of US-origin weapons systems (missiles, aircraft, electronic warfare) routed through the RBA. All pass-through entries are excluded from supplier analysis in this report.
3. Contract amendments carry valuation uncertainty
AusTender does not reliably link contract amendments to originals. A contract escalating from $10M to $50M through successive amendments may appear as multiple discrete records. Total spend per supplier can therefore be either understated (if the original record dominates) or overstated (if amendments are separately counted). Large infrastructure and managed services contracts, which attract frequent amendments, carry the highest valuation uncertainty in this dataset.
4. Commonwealth only — state procurement not captured
This analysis covers Commonwealth agencies exclusively. State procurement (Defence SA, state police forces, emergency services, and state-run defence precincts) operates on entirely separate registers. For companies active in the South Australian defence industry or seeking state-level contracts, a separate extraction from individual state portals is required.
5. Contract descriptions are deliberately uninformative
Titles such as "Support Services", "ICT Services", and "Labour Support Services" are standard across the dataset. The AusTender record alone cannot determine what was procured. Scope analysis requires cross-referencing tender documents (where published), ministerial disclosures, or industry sources. For sensitive contracts, particularly within Defence, descriptions are often deliberately opaque.
6. The $80k threshold defines the floor of analytical relevance
Below the $80k Commonwealth limited tender threshold, agencies procure direct with minimal process. The 128,879 contracts below that level are a record of routine operational activity. Filtering to contracts above $1M yields 13,952 contracts covering $200.1B (88% of total value), a dataset that is tractable, strategically meaningful, and where competitive dynamics operate.
Methodology

Source. AusTender OCDS API (api.tenders.gov.au/ocds), the authoritative Commonwealth procurement register. Every award notice published by every agency is captured with no manual filtering at collection.

Period. 1 July 2022 – 6 April 2026: FY2023, FY2024, and FY2025 in full; FY2026 to date of extraction. Contracts are assigned to financial years by award date (1 Jul – 30 Jun).

Processing. Records are loaded into a local SQLite database. Supplier names are normalised across case, punctuation, and legal suffix variants to prevent artificial fragmentation of spend. All values are as-published awarded amounts in AUD.

Exclusions. Three pass-through accounts are removed from all supplier-level analysis: FMS Account Reserve Bank of Australia ($23.3B, the US Foreign Military Sales channel), Cooperative Programs Account, and direct RBA entries. These are government-to-government payment mechanisms. Agency-level totals retain all contracts regardless of supplier type.
